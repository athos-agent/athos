---
id: build-the-argument-of-a-brief
title: Build the argument of a brief for its audience
when: preparing a brief, pleading, answer or memo meant to convince a judge, an opposing party or a client; ordering the arguments or checking whether a draft persuades
---

## #audience
**When:** before writing a line of the brief.
**Do:** ask and write down who it is for (judge, court, opposing party, client), what they already believe and the exact decision asked of them. If it is unclear, ask the person; never assume it.
**Because:** you argue for a concrete audience; a miscalibrated argument produces the opposite effect.
**Links:** [[build-the-argument-of-a-brief#premises]]

## #premises
**When:** the audience is known.
**Do:** list in three columns what the audience already accepts: undisputed facts (with their proof in the record), presumptions (what is normal unless proven otherwise) and shared values. Mark the disputed ones: they are not a starting point, they are what must be proven.
**Because:** every argument starts from prior agreement; a disputed fact used as a premise weakens everything built on it.
**Links:** [[build-the-argument-of-a-brief#techniques]]

## #techniques
**When:** the premises are clear.
**Do:** for each thesis, choose the technique and name it: rule of justice or precedent (like cases, like treatment), pragmatic argument (consequences), authority (doctrine, case law), example or analogy, or dissociation (letter vs. spirit, appearance vs. reality). Note each one's risk (a forced analogy, a contested authority).
**Because:** naming the technique shows what holds it up and where the other side will attack.
**Links:** [[build-the-argument-of-a-brief#rebuttal]]

## #rebuttal
**When:** your own arguments are chosen.
**Do:** write the two or three strongest objections of the other side and answer them before they are raised; use what the other side already admitted in the record.
**Because:** an anticipated objection loses force; the opponent's own admission weighs more than any new argument.
**Links:** [[build-the-argument-of-a-brief#order]]

## #order
**When:** structuring the brief.
**Do:** put the strongest argument first and last, the weaker ones in the middle, and cut those that add nothing: quantity does not replace strength. Facts before law; a concrete request.
**Because:** order shapes persuasion, and an excessive argument can turn against you.
**Links:** [[build-the-argument-of-a-brief#review]]

## #review
**When:** the draft is ready.
**Do:** reread it as the judge and mark every adjective that qualifies without proving, every claim without a cite to the record, and every excess of tone. Strategy decisions (what to ask, concede or leave unsaid) are the person's: propose them, never make them.
**Because:** excessive judgments mostly judge whoever makes them; and professional responsibility lies with whoever signs.
